# Humanize

Write for the reader and purpose. Prefer concrete facts, natural rhythm, and the author's intended voice. Remove generic padding and formulaic phrasing. Do not write to fool AI detectors or claim that a style proves who wrote a text.

The source guide, [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), is descriptive. Its examples are clues, not proof or universal rules. Human writers use some of the same forms. Avoid the patterns below when they add no precise meaning. Keep a word, structure, punctuation mark, citation, or format when the task, source, author, language, accessibility need, or house style requires it. Preserve exact quotations, code, identifiers, legal text, and user wording unless asked to edit them.

## Workflow

1. Identify the audience, purpose, format, author voice, and factual basis. Ask only when a missing choice would change the result.
2. Draft the useful content first. Use details supported by the source or supplied context. Do not invent anecdotes, local customs, citations, or personal experience to make prose feel vivid.
3. Replace vague or inflated claims with the concrete fact. Remove commentary that only repeats or praises the point.
4. Read the whole piece aloud or review it for varied sentence length, clear links between ideas, and a voice suited to the author. Avoid deliberate quirks added just to sound human.
5. Run the applicable checks below. For ordinary prose, use the prose checks. Use the MediaWiki checks only when editing Wikipedia or another MediaWiki artifact. Fix factual, attribution, and citation defects; do not just hide their surface signs.
6. Preserve requested structure and formatting. Do not add a conclusion, table, section, citation, or explanation only to make the response look complete.

## Voice and specificity

- Write like a person speaking to a person, not a corporate announcement. Be direct and confident. Use active voice when it keeps the actor clear. Keep words such as “maybe” or “I think” when they express real uncertainty.
- Address the reader as “you” when the text speaks to an external audience. Use contractions when they fit the author's register.
- Replace praise with a fact, example, constraint, or measured result. Put users, customers, and community members before company self-praise. Use realistic examples, not `foo`, `bar`, or `baz`, when the prose calls for an example.
- Make titles specific and useful. Draft a working title, finish the piece, then revise the title to fit what the text actually delivers. Avoid clickbait.
- Use plain alternatives when they are accurate: “help” for “assistance,” “proven approaches” for “best practices,” a measured speed claim for “blazing fast/lightning fast,” “start” for “commence,” “go into” for “delve,” “help/ease” for “facilitate,” “do/build/add” for “implement” when it is not a technical term, “person” for “individual,” “first” for “initial,” “use” for “leverage/utilize,” “important” for “mission-critical,” “many” for “numerous,” “called” for “referred to as,” “rest” for “remainder,” “enough” for “sufficient,” and a concrete noun instead of “thing.” Describe what happens automatically instead of calling it “seamless.” Keep technical terms and exact meanings.
- Remove empty modifiers such as “a bit,” “pretty,” “quite,” “rather,” or “very” only when they add no meaning. Remove “actually,” “arguably,” “it seems,” “sort of,” “kind of,” and “pretty much” unless they mark a real qualification.
- Do not use “game-changing,” “innovative,” “modern,” “robust,” or “great” as unsupported praise. Give a measured speed or reliability claim, or name the specific benefit.
- Keep emotional clarity, rhythm, and imagery when they sharpen the point. Use them to sharpen hooks and transitions, not to decorate or generalize a concrete claim. Preserve the author's voice. In translation, preserve meaning, register, domain terms, implied audience, and formatting; prefer natural target-language phrasing over word-for-word syntax. Flag a translation choice only when alternatives materially change the meaning.

## Prose checks

### Content and claims

- Remove unearned claims about a subject's significance, legacy, role in broader trends, or place in an “evolving landscape.” Watch for stock claims such as “stands/serves as,” “a testament/reminder,” “crucial/pivotal/key,” “underscores its significance,” “reflects broader,” “enduring/lasting,” “sets the stage,” “marks a shift/turning point,” “focal point,” “indelible mark,” and “deeply rooted.” Keep them only when the specific claim is supported and useful.
- Do not advertise notability by listing types of media, outlets, or experts. Avoid boilerplate about “independent coverage,” “regional/national media,” “trade publications,” “written by a leading expert,” “featured in,” or “active social media presence.” State a relevant, verified source or fact directly when it matters.
- Remove superficial analysis attached to a fact through a trailing “-ing” phrase. Check claims that something is “highlighting,” “ensuring,” “reflecting,” “symbolizing,” “contributing,” “fostering,” “encompassing,” or “enhancing” an impact, and phrases like “valuable insights” or “aligns/resonates with.” Say what happened and who says so.
- Remove promotional or advertisement-like language, including “boasts,” “vibrant,” “rich,” “profound,” “showcasing,” “exemplifies,” “groundbreaking,” “renowned,” “diverse array,” “natural beauty,” “nestled,” “in the heart of,” and claims of “commitment.” Avoid the cultural-heritage and press-release variants unless the genre calls for them and the claim is sourced.
- Name the actual relationship between people, places, and events. Replace vague “connected with,” “associated with,” or “in connection to” wording with the role, action, or link that the evidence supports.
- Attribute opinions to a specific source. Avoid vague authorities such as “experts,” “observers,” “industry reports,” “critics,” and “several sources” when the source is unnamed or the number is small. Do not use “such as” to introduce a list that is actually exhaustive.
- Avoid stock endings that announce challenges, legacy, or future prospects (“Despite these challenges,” “Challenges and Legacy,” “Future Outlook”) without useful, sourced content. End when the requested information is complete.
- Do not add an “Awards and recognition” section by default. Include awards only when relevant, verified, and requested by the format.
- Check for bias in selection and framing. Do not default to pro-authoritarian framing or give state power undue legitimacy. Describe contested claims and their sources accurately.

### Language and sentence shape

- Avoid clusters of fashionable “AI vocabulary,” not every occurrence of an individual word. Review unnecessary uses of “additionally,” “align,” “boast,” “bolster,” “challenge,” “clearer,” “crucial,” “deep dive,” “delve,” “dependable,” “echoed,” “emphasize,” “enduring,” “enhance,” “foster,” “garner,” “highlight,” “interplay,” “intricate,” “key,” “landscape,” “leverage,” “matters,” “meticulous,” “multifaceted,” “pivotal,” “practical,” “prioritize,” “quietly,” “robust,” “showcase,” “steady,” “tapestry,” “testament,” “underscore,” “universally,” “valuable,” and “vibrant.” Also check shallow “scientific” terms such as “causal,” “empirical,” and “correlate” when used without evidence. Use a plain, exact alternative when it says the same thing. Keep a term when it is literal or technically correct. Do not force every sentence into a fixed 8–20 word range or use one punctuation pattern throughout.
- Use ordinary “is,” “are,” and “has” when they are the clearest verbs. Do not contort a sentence to avoid them with “serves as,” “stands as,” “marks,” “functions as,” “operates as,” “represents,” “boasts,” “features,” “maintains,” “offers,” or “refers to.”
- Avoid canned negative parallels: “not just X, but also Y,” “not X, but Y,” and “Y rather than X.” Use a direct statement unless the contrast is essential.
- Do not turn a list label or broad title into a proper noun unless it is an actual name. Apply normal capitalization to list names and generic topics.
- Do not force every explanation into exactly three points. Use the number of examples or steps the content needs.

### Voice and communication

- Remove service-script openers and closers such as “Of course!”, “Certainly!”, “You're absolutely right!”, “I hope this helps,” “Let me know,” “Would you like…,” “Is there anything else…,” and “Here is a more detailed breakdown” when they add no value. Answer the request directly.
- Do not announce that you are writing “a Wikipedia-style article” or describe a conversion the user did not request. Provide the requested artifact in its target form.
- Skip “Great question,” “You're right,” “Let me help,” and “Let's dive in” as default openers. Avoid broad scene-setting such as “In today's fast-paced world” or “In the ever-evolving landscape,” and clichés such as “The future of X,” “By developers, for developers,” or “We obsess over X.” Remove stock excitement, self-congratulation, and “It's not just X, it's Y” when they add no specific meaning.
- Remove boilerplate about the availability or recommended use of sources: “based on available information,” “specific details are limited,” “not widely documented,” or “treat this as X rather than Y.” State the actual evidence and uncertainty where they affect the answer. Do not hide genuine limits.
- Replace phrasal templates and placeholders with completed, specific prose. Do not leave bracketed prompts, generic names, or scaffold language in a final piece unless the user asked for a template.
- Do not add procedural self-praise (“I preserved,” “I avoided,” “I ensured”) to a finished text unless the user asked for an editing report.
- Keep the author's register and language variety. Do not make a sudden shift into corporate, academic, promotional, or overly ornate language.
- Avoid perfectly symmetrical paragraphs and lists. Use transitions such as “furthermore,” “additionally,” or “moreover” only when they show a real relationship. Remove hedge stacks and empty citation placeholders. Check for copied artifacts, nonbreaking spaces, and stray Unicode punctuation, but preserve characters that the target format or source requires.

## Format checks

Apply only when the requested format uses these elements. Formatting patterns are not proof of AI authorship.

- Use sentence case by default. Use title case only when the genre or house style calls for it.
- Use bold for navigation or emphasis, not as decoration or for every list label. Avoid repeating vertical lists of “**Label:** explanation” when a normal list or prose is clearer.
- Keep heading levels sequential. Do not duplicate a page title as a body heading, skip levels, use repeated level-one headings, or create a parent heading with only subheadings and no useful content.
- Do not use emoji as structural markers unless the user or established format calls for them.
- Use a table only when rows and columns make comparison easier. Do not convert ordinary prose or a simple list into a table.
- Use quotation marks and apostrophes consistently with the requested locale or house style. Curly punctuation alone is a weak signal; do not strip it mechanically.
- Use Oxford commas consistently when the destination style permits. Keep exclamation marks sparse. Start with “But” or “And” only when that improves the flow; do not repeat the device. Break long comma chains into sentences when that makes the meaning clearer. Keep headings short and use sentence case unless the house style says otherwise.
- Use Markdown only where supported and needed. Check for malformed asterisks, heading marks, unusual links, and decorative thematic breaks between every section. Use the target platform's actual syntax.
- Do not include broken wikitext, internal formatting or reference markup errors, or exposed model/tool artifacts. Check for ChatGPT `contentReference`, `oaicite`/`oai_citation`, `+1`, `turn0search0`, `attributableIndex`; Gemini `[cite: n]` and `[span_n](start_span)`; Grok `grok_card`/`grok_render_citation_card_json`; DeepSeek lenticular brackets or dagger markers; Perplexity `attached_file` or `ppl-ai-file-upload`; and stray `:::writing` labels. Remove an artifact only after confirming it is not literal source content.
- Do not invent categories, templates, or links to searches. Confirm that a category/template exists and fits the target page. Do not use “links to searches” as a substitute for a source.

## MediaWiki and citation checks

Use this section only for Wikipedia or another MediaWiki task. It covers platform-specific signs that do not belong in ordinary prose.

- Do not add an AI-style edit summary. Avoid canned claims that the edit “ensured” compliance with policy or improved neutrality, verifiability, tone, clarity, or flow; claims that information was “preserved,” “retained,” or mistakes “avoided”; and procedural summaries that overstate added or verified citations. Describe the actual edit briefly.
- Do not pad an edit summary with template or parameter names, markup details, or assurances about citation quality. Do not claim an AfC review or approval that did not occur.
- Check external links. Remove tracking parameters such as `utm_source` or `referrer` when not needed and verify that a link resolves to the intended page.
- Verify DOI and ISBN values and confirm that a DOI leads to the cited work. Do not invent book page numbers, URLs, or bibliographic data. Give book citations enough location detail for the requested style.
- Follow the target wiki's reference conventions. Check for malformed or unconventional references and for named references declared in a references section but never used in the article body.
- Do not insert search-result citation tokens, dead links, or unverified claims to make an article appear sourced.
- For talk-page comments, do not paste AI-style policy shortcuts, maintenance-banner transclusions, long generic headings, defensive claims about human effort, generic feedback requests, dismissals of concerns, or customer-service language. Answer the specific issue in plain language and cite a policy only when it applies.
- For AfC drafts and user pages, do not add canned submission statements, pre-placed maintenance templates, or boilerplate user-page sections. Do not make repeated, low-value edits to game permissions or imply reviewer acceptance or authority that has not been granted. Follow the actual workflow and policies.

## Historical and weak indicators

The source page marks these as historical, weak, or context-dependent. Treat them as review prompts, not automatic errors:

- Old knowledge-cutoff lines such as “up to my last training update” or “as of my last knowledge update” are usually irrelevant. State the date and source of a real limitation instead.
- Didactic disclaimers such as “it's important to note/remember” and repeated warnings like “may vary” often add little. Keep a qualification when it changes the decision or prevents a factual error.
- Do not add section summaries (“In summary,” “In conclusion,” “Overall”) that repeat the section. A conclusion is appropriate when the requested format needs one.
- Do not use canned prompt refusals (“as an AI language model”) in ordinary content. If a limitation must be stated, state it plainly and specifically.
- Em dashes are not inherently an AI sign. Avoid excessive or repetitive punctuation, but use a dash when it fits the sentence and house style.
- Check for abrupt cutoffs and unfinished sentences before delivery. Do not mistake a deliberate short answer for a cutoff.
- Verify access dates when a citation style requires them; old or copied access dates can be stale.
- Do not use thesaurus substitutions or “elegant variation” to avoid repeating a precise term. Repeat the accurate term when that is clearer.
- Do not treat perfect grammar, mixed casual/formal registers, “robotic,” academic or formal prose, a lone transition word, missing citations, or strange/correct wikitext as reliable proof of AI authorship. Strange wikitext can come from editing tools; correct wikitext is normal. Do not use AI detectors or stylistic judgment alone to accuse an author.

The page also lists possible signs of human editing: text predating ChatGPT, an author's ability to explain editorial choices, and direct/simple syntax. Its syntax examples include basic “is/are/has” phrasing, supported superlatives or definite claims, natural qualifiers such as “very,” “perhaps,” or “tends to,” and occasional wordy constructions such as “in order to” or “as a result of.” These can provide context, not proof. Do not imitate supposed “human tells,” but do not remove valid examples of these forms just because they appear on that list.

## Final pass

Before returning the text, ask:

- Does each sentence add a fact, explanation, or useful transition?
- Are the claims specific, supported, and correctly attributed?
- Did I add significance, praise, a three-part list, a generic conclusion, or a source disclaimer without a reason?
- Did I use a stock opener, rhetorical contrast, repeated transition, artificially balanced paragraph, or a pile of synonyms?
- Is any sentence using a listed phrase as filler or a vague substitute for a real relationship?
- Are headings, tables, emphasis, punctuation, Markdown, references, and edit summaries appropriate to this format?
- Did I preserve the author's voice and exact text where accuracy requires it?
